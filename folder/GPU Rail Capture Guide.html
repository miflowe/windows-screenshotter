Part 1

What you're actually measuring

A rail is one power supply line feeding the GPU. A board has several: the core rail (often under 1 V), memory, a 1.8 V auxiliary rail, the 12 V input, and so on. Each rail has a voltage (how hard it pushes) and a current (how much the GPU is drawing).

Powering on is a sequence. The rails turn on in a set order. Each one reports “power good” (a signal called PGOOD) once it's stable. Then reset releases, firmware runs, the driver loads, and workloads start. Power-off is the same story in reverse.

A spike (also called a transient or glitch) is a brief jump or dip on a rail. Many last nanoseconds to microseconds. They matter because a dip below spec can crash or reset the GPU, an overshoot can stress parts, and they tend to happen at specific moments: a rail switching on, or the instant a workload starts pulling current.

The core challenge in one sentence. You want to watch for a long time (seconds for a boot, hours for workloads) and see events that last a few nanoseconds. No single instrument does both forever, so this system combines tools so that each covers the other's blind spot.
Part 2

Sampling rate, from zero

The single idea everything else builds on.

A digital scope doesn't record a continuous line. It takes rapid snapshots of the voltage, called samples, and joins the dots on screen.

  • Sample rate is how many snapshots per second. Your MSO64B tops out at 25 GS/s (25 billion per second, one every 0.04 ns).
  • Record length (memory) is how many snapshots fit in one capture. Your scope has 62.5 million standard, with options for 125M, 250M, 500M or 1 billion. Check which one your unit has.

Here's the trade. The memory is a fixed number of slots. If you record for longer, the scope has to space the snapshots further apart to make them fit. Think of a flipbook with a fixed number of pages: if it has to cover a whole day instead of one second, each page is a bigger jump in time.

gap between samples = capture length ÷ record length
Flip it to get sample rate: record length ÷ capture length.

Worked example. A 2-second boot on 62.5 million points: 2 s ÷ 62,500,000 = 32 ns between snapshots. A spike shorter than 32 ns can fit entirely between two of them. At full speed (25 GS/s), those same 62.5 million points last only 2.5 ms, far too short for a boot.

Part 4

Bandwidth: how fast your “eyes” are

A different problem from sample rate, and both must be good enough.

Sample rate is how often you take a snapshot. Bandwidth is how fast a change the signal path can follow at all. With infinitely fast snapshots and a slow probe, you're still photographing a blurry signal.

Think of the signal path as a pipe. A narrow pipe can't pass sudden changes. A sharp spike comes out the other end shorter and wider. It doesn't vanish, it just looks smaller than it was. That's arguably worse than missing it, because you look at the result and think “fine, only 40 mV.”

fastest edge you can see ≈ 0.35 ÷ bandwidth
Anything sharper than this gets rounded off and reads smaller than it really is.

About your 20 MHz setting

Recommendation: turn the 20 MHz limit off while recording, and apply 20 MHz filtering in software afterward. You can always filter later, but you can never unfilter. One full-bandwidth capture gives you both views:

  • Filtered (20 MHz) view for clean ripple numbers. Many power-supply ripple specs are defined at 20 MHz, so keep this for spec comparisons.
  • Raw (full-bandwidth) view for spike hunting and worst-case peaks.

Each report can then show two numbers per rail, for example “ripple 18 mV (20 MHz) / worst-case peak +62 mV (full BW).”

Check what “my probe is 20 MHz” means. If it's the scope's bandwidth-limit setting, just change it (the script command is CH<x>:BANdwidth). If the probe itself is rated 20 MHz, which is common for some current and differential probes, no setting will help. That probe physically can't see fast spikes. Keep it for slow, accurate measurements and use a faster probe for spike hunting. The probe's model number is printed on it; look up its datasheet.

Dealing with the extra noise. Full bandwidth looks fuzzier, and Peak Detect makes the fuzz look thicker. To manage it without hiding anything real:

  • Fix the source. A lot of “noise” on rail measurements is pickup from long probe ground leads. A short ground connection or a probe designed for power rails cuts it down. A long ground lead can also create fake spikes from ringing.
  • Zoom the vertical scale onto the rail. Use offset so a 0.9 V rail fills the screen around 0.9 V, not 0–12 V. Leave headroom so big spikes don't run off the top of the screen and get clipped.
  • Try the middle steps. The scope has bandwidth-limit options between 20 MHz and full. A middle step can cut a lot of noise and still show real spikes.

The test that settles it. Capture the same event (a boot, a workload start) once at 20 MHz and once at full bandwidth, per rail. If the peaks match, the filter wasn't hiding anything on that rail. If full bandwidth shows bigger peaks, it was. Record the result in the tool's config so the choice is deliberate.

How the current is measured matters too

GPU core rails can carry very large currents through copper planes on the board, so you often can't clip a current probe around them. Common options each have their own speed limit:

MethodHow it worksSpeed
Clamp current probeClips around a wire or cable, like the 12 V input cableCheck the probe spec; often tens to ~100 MHz
Shunt resistor + diff probeMeasure the tiny voltage across a known resistor in the pathCan be fast; limited by the diff probe
VRM current monitor (IMON)The voltage regulator outputs a voltage proportional to currentUsually slow and filtered; good for trends, not spikes

Ask your hardware team which method each rail uses. That answer sets the real limit for current spikes, no matter how the scope is configured.

Part 5

Scope vs DAQ, and why you want both

A DAQ (data acquisition system) is a box with many input channels that records slowly but continuously, straight to disk, for as long as you like.

The easiest way to picture the two:

  • The scope is a high-speed camera with a small memory card. It sees incredibly fast events, but only for a short clip.
  • The DAQ is a security camera recording all day at a low frame rate. It never stops, but blinks right past anything quick.
Scope (MSO64B)DAQ
SpeedVery fast, catches ns eventsMuch slower
How long it recordsLimited by memory, then gaps while it re-armsStreams to disk for hours
Channels4 analog hereOften dozens, cheap per channel
Fast spikesGood with Peak DetectPoor many DAQs filter fast content out on purpose
Best atThe detail of fast eventsThe whole timeline: sequence order, slow sag, temperature, every rail
So would a DAQ solve everything? No. It solves the “record forever” problem and is worse at spikes. The scope solves the spike problem and can't record forever. Together they cover each other. Specialized “streaming digitizers” do both but are expensive, and the bandwidth rules still apply.
Part 6

How it's wired

The scope and DAQ do not share a probe. Each connects to the board on its own.

  1. Scope probes. Differential probes on the 3–4 rails you care most about, plus one current measurement. Connect at the rail's sense points near the GPU, not at the power connector, because big currents cause voltage drops along the board's copper.
  2. DAQ wires. The DAQ gets its own wires to every rail, and to the sequencing signals (enables, power-good, reset). Two instruments on the same point is fine: both only listen, and barely draw any current.
  3. The marker wire. When the scope triggers, it sends a pulse out of the AUX OUT connector on its back panel (you set what that output does in the scope's settings). The pulse goes into a spare DAQ input. Every scope capture becomes a mark on the DAQ timeline, so you can say “spike at 14:32:07, during workload X” without trusting two clocks to agree.
  4. The workload marker. When the test software starts or stops a workload, it toggles a spare GPIO into the DAQ (or at least writes a timestamp). Now every event is tagged with what the GPU was doing.
  5. The test PC. Talks to both instruments over USB or Ethernet, runs the workloads on the GPU's host, saves all files, and runs the analysis.

Gotchas

  • The marker pulse can be too short for the DAQ. This is the sampling lesson again: a 1 µs pulse into a DAQ sampling every 10 µs can be missed. Use a DAQ digital input that detects edges (many timestamp edges of any length), or stretch the pulse with a small circuit.
  • Ground loops. Scope, DAQ, board and PC all connected together can create extra ground paths that add hum and noise. Use one common ground point, and prefer a DAQ with isolated inputs.
Part 7

The capture plan

The whole system, mapped onto the GPU's lifecycle.

One scope, one setup at a time. The scope can't run the overview capture and the event trigger simultaneously. The tool switches between them: overview during the boot, event mode for idle, workloads and shutdown, and zoom-ins on repeat boots once you know where to look.
DAQ timelinealways on, every rail, hours
  • Records every rail, the sequencing signals, the workload marker and the scope marker, continuously.
  • Answers: did the rails come up in the right order? Did a rail sag slowly under load? When did each scope capture happen, and during what?
Scope overview captureonce per boot
  • One long capture covering the whole boot window. Peak Detect, maximum record length, full bandwidth (or the per-rail value you tested), 16-bit transfer, and the full record pulled off the scope.
  • Answers: every excursion's height and roughly when it happened during boot. Loses exact shape.
Scope event captureidle, workloads, shutdown
  • A window trigger on each rail's tolerance (for example ±5%): the scope fires whenever the rail leaves that band.
  • FastFrame stores many short full-speed snapshots back to back, each with its own timestamp, with very short re-arm time between them.
  • Every trigger also pulses the marker wire, so each event appears on the DAQ timeline.
Scope zoom-in capturerepeat boots
  • When the overview shows something odd at, say, 340 ms into boot, trigger on a sequencing signal (PWR_EN, PGOOD, a GPIO) with a horizontal delay, and capture just that region at full speed.
  • Answers: the exact shape of the spike, which tells you what caused it.

What this still can't do (say this out loud in the presentation)

  • Faster than the probe. Nothing past the probe's bandwidth reaches the scope, whatever the settings.
  • Gap-free full speed for hours. A scope isn't a streaming recorder. In long runs, the scope only keeps full-speed detail of excursions that break the tolerance window. Anything that stays inside tolerance isn't captured at full speed, which is by design: it didn't violate spec. The DAQ still sees the slow picture.
  • Blind time while transferring. While the scope is sending a large capture to the PC, it isn't watching. The tool should log those periods so they're known, not hidden.
  • File sizes. At 2 bytes per point, a 62.5 M capture is 125 MB per channel; a 1 G capture is 2 GB per channel. Transfers take real time and storage adds up.
Part 8

Prove it works with a fake spike

Reading code isn't proof. Feeding in a known spike is.

  1. Make a known spike. A narrow pulse (20–50 ns) on top of a DC level, from a pulse generator, or from the scope's built-in function generator if your unit has that option.
  2. Feed it into the scope through the same kind of probe and channel settings you use on the board.
  3. Run the full automation at the real boot-length timebase, exactly as it will run on the board.
  4. Check the saved file, not the screen. The spike should appear at the right time and close to its real height.
  5. If it's missing or short, something in the chain is losing events. Use Part 9 to find which stage. Repeat until it passes, then rerun this test whenever the script changes.

Questions you'll probably get

Why not just turn the sample rate all the way up?

Because memory is fixed. At 25 GS/s the standard memory fills in 2.5 ms, and a boot takes far longer. Peak Detect is how you get long captures without losing spikes.

Why not just use a DAQ?

DAQs record forever but are slow, and many filter fast content out on purpose. They'd miss the very spikes we're hunting. The scope covers speed, the DAQ covers time.

Doesn't full bandwidth make everything noisy?

It looks fuzzier, yes. We fix noise at the source (short probe grounds), filter in software for ripple numbers, and keep the raw data for peaks. We also tested each rail at 20 MHz vs full to see whether the filter was hiding anything.

What can this system still miss?

Anything faster than the probe can see; excursions during workloads that stay inside tolerance (not recorded at full speed, by design); and anything during the short periods when the scope is transferring data, which the tool logs.

How do we know it actually works?

We inject a known narrow pulse and run the whole pipeline. If the pulse appears in the saved file at the right height and time, the chain works. We rerun this whenever the script changes.

Part 12

Find these out first

Each one fills a bracket in the brief.

  • Which memory option is installed on your scope (62.5M standard, or 125M up to 1G). It's shown in the scope's installed-options screen.
  • Every probe's model number and rated bandwidth, especially current and differential probes.
  • Whether “20 MHz” is the scope setting or the probe's rating.
  • The list of rails, each one's nominal voltage, tolerance, and where its sense point is.
  • How current is measured on each rail (clamp, shunt, or VRM monitor). Ask the hardware team.
  • How long a boot takes, roughly, from power applied to driver loaded.
  • Whether you have a DAQ, which model, how many channels, and whether it has edge-detecting digital inputs and isolated channels.
  • A way to make a test pulse: a pulse generator, or the scope's function generator option.
Reference

Glossary

Rail
One power supply line feeding the GPU, like the 0.9 V core rail.
Spike / transient
A brief jump or dip on a rail, often nanoseconds to microseconds long.
Sample
One snapshot of the voltage at one instant.
Sample rate
Snapshots per second. GS/s = billions per second, MS/s = millions.
Record length
How many samples fit in one capture. The scope's memory.
Acquisition mode
What the scope keeps from each gap between saved points: Sample, Peak Detect, High Res, Average.
Peak Detect
Keeps the highest and lowest value in every gap, so short spikes survive long captures.
Bandwidth
How fast a change the signal path can follow. Measured in MHz or GHz.
Bandwidth limit
A filter in the scope (like 20 MHz) that removes fast content before it's recorded.
ADC
The part of the scope that turns voltage into numbers. The MSO64B's is 12-bit.
Differential probe
Measures the voltage between two points, neither of which needs to be ground.
Sense point
The spot on the board where a rail's voltage is meant to be measured, close to the GPU.
DAQ
Data acquisition system. Many slow channels that record continuously to disk.
Trigger
The condition that tells the scope when to capture.
Window trigger
Fires when a signal leaves a band, like ±5% around the rail's nominal voltage.
FastFrame
Tektronix feature that stores many short triggered captures back to back, each with a timestamp.
AUX OUT
Back-panel scope output that can send a pulse every time it triggers.
PGOOD / EN / RESET
Board signals for “rail is stable,” “turn this rail on,” and “hold the chip in reset.”
SCPI
The text command language scripts use to control the scope, like ACQuire:MODe PEAKdetect.
Ground loop
Extra ground paths between connected equipment that add hum and noise.

MSO64B figures (25 GS/s maximum, 62.5 M points standard, options to 1 G points, 12-bit ADC) come from Tektronix's published specifications. Confirm them against your own unit's installed options and the MSO64B programmer manual.
