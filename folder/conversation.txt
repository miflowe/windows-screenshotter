Why zooming out risks missing spikes

The scope has fixed memory. Capture Duration = Memory ÷ Sample Rate. The MSO64B has 62.5 Mpoints standard, with 125, 250, 500 Mpoints, or 1 Gpoints optional, and a top rate of 25 GS/s with 4 channels. At full speed, standard memory holds only about 2.5 ms.

datatec

To fit a 2-second boot into 62.5 Mpts, the scope stores about one point every 32 ns. With the 1 Gpt option it’s about one every 2 ns.

What happens to everything between those stored points depends on the acquisition mode:

Sample mode (the default): It retains the first sampled point from each acquisition interval and throws the rest away. A 10 ns spike between two stored points is gone, and nothing tells you it happened. This is the failure you’re worried about. 
manualshive
Peak Detect: It retains the highest and lowest values of all the samples in two consecutive acquisition intervals. The ADC keeps sampling fast and saves the extremes. You lose the spike’s exact shape, but its amplitude and rough time survive. Tek describes it as useful for catching high frequency glitches. For a full-sequence capture, this is the key setting. 
manualshive
High Res mode: It uses FIR filtering based on the current sample rate. That lowers noise, but it also smooths out narrow spikes. An AI chasing “clean-looking waveforms” might pick this, and it works against you. 
manualshive
Average mode: It averages across many captures. Random transients get averaged away, so it’s the worst choice here.

What a solid design looks like
Overview capture: Full boot window, maximum record length, Peak Detect, a 2-byte (16-bit) transfer, and the full record pulled. This tells you about every excursion and roughly when it happened.
Event capture: Trigger on a window trigger set to each rail’s tolerance (e.g. ±5%), and use FastFrame, which runs at >5,000,000 wfms/s. Every violation gets a short full-rate snapshot with a timestamp. Even when you aren’t recording everything, the scope still catches anything that breaks the limits. 
valuetronics
Zoom-in on later boots: Once the overview shows something odd at, say, t = 340 ms, trigger off a sequencing signal (PWR_EN, PGOOD, a GPIO) with a horizontal delay. Then capture just that region at full rate.

Two limits worth knowing:

Long workloads (minutes to hours) can’t be recorded gap-free. A scope isn’t a streaming recorder. Repeated captures have dead time between them, so the trigger in step 2 is what protects you there.
The probe sets the real limit. If your current probe’s bandwidth is around 100 MHz, a 5 ns current spike never reaches the scope, whatever its settings. Bandwidth-limit settings on the channels (e.g. 20 MHz for ripple) also flatten fast edges.

If it doesn’t, the pipeline is losing events somewhere, and you’ve found that out before it costs you a debug.

Some things to notice as you play with it:

In Sample mode, whether you catch the spike is mostly luck. At some gap settings a snapshot happens to land on the spike and it shows up. Move the slider a little and it disappears. You can’t build a debug tool on luck.
Peak detect fixes this. The scope keeps taking snapshots at full speed internally, but it doesn’t save all of them. For each gap it saves just two numbers: the highest and lowest voltage it saw. So even with snapshots spread far apart, the spike’s peak still gets saved. You lose the spike’s exact shape, but you know how high it went and roughly when.

The two numbers to keep in your head:

Sampling rate = snapshots per second (or, flipped around, the time gap between them)
Gap between snapshots = how long you’re recording ÷ how many snapshots fit in memory

Record longer and the gaps get bigger. Buying more memory (the 1 Gpt option) makes them smaller again. Peak detect makes sure big gaps don’t hide spikes.

That’s why the first item on my checklist was checking whether your script sets the scope to Peak Detect. If it’s left on Sample mode, the script has the problem you’re worried about.

Probe bandwidth: how fast your “eyes” are

Sampling rate is how often the scope takes a snapshot. Bandwidth is how fast a change the signal path can follow at all. Even with infinitely fast snapshots, a slow probe means you’re photographing a blurry signal.

Think of the probe as a pipe the signal travels through on its way to the scope. A slow pipe can’t pass sudden changes. A sharp spike comes out the other end shorter and wider, rounded off. It doesn’t vanish, but it reads as smaller than it really was. That can be worse than missing it, because you look at it and think “that’s fine, only 30 mV.”

The signal passes through several stages, and the slowest one sets your limit:

rail → probe → scope input → bandwidth-limit setting → ADC

Rule of thumb: the fastest edge a stage can show is about 0.35 ÷ bandwidth. A 100 MHz probe can’t show anything sharper than about 3.5 ns. So a 2 ns spike would be blurred and understated.

Three things to check:

Your current probe. Current probes are usually much slower than voltage probes. Look up its bandwidth spec.
The scope’s bandwidth-limit setting. People often turn on a 20 MHz limit to make rails look “cleaner.” That filter blurs spikes the same way a slow probe does. Check whether your script sets it.
The probe’s ground lead. A long ground clip can make the probe ring and show spikes that aren’t really on the rail. Probes made for measuring power rails, with short ground connections, avoid this. You want to neither miss real spikes nor chase fake ones.

Would a DAQ solve everything?

No. It solves a different problem, and it’s actually worse at spikes.

	Scope	DAQ
Speed	Very fast, catches ns events	Much slower
How long it can record	Limited by memory, then gaps	Streams to disk for hours
Channels	4 here	Often dozens, cheap per channel
Fast spikes	Good, with Peak Detect	Poor: many DAQs have filters that deliberately smooth spikes out

The DAQ’s strength is that it never stops recording. That’s exactly the scope’s weakness on long workloads. So the strong setup is to use both:

DAQ: the whole timeline, continuously. Power-on order, slow sag under load, temperatures, every rail, for hours.
Scope: fast stuff, with Peak Detect for overview captures and a window trigger that fires whenever a rail goes out of tolerance.
Link them: feed the scope’s trigger output into a spare DAQ channel. Then every scope capture shows up as a marker on the DAQ’s timeline, so you can see “spike at 14:32:07, during workload X.”

There are high-end “streaming digitizers” that sit in between: fast and continuous. They’re expensive, and the bandwidth rules above still apply.

Bottom line: no single instrument records everything, forever, at full speed. What you can achieve is that every spike is either captured in detail or at least flagged with a timestamp, so nothing slips by silently. The scope’s trigger plus a DAQ timeline gets you there.

Keep the 20 MHz filter off while recording, and apply it in software afterward.

Why: you can filter later, but you can’t unfilter

A 20 MHz limit removes the fast content before the scope digitizes it. Once that’s done, no software can bring it back. If you record at full bandwidth instead, you can apply a 20 MHz filter to the saved data in Python whenever you want. One capture gives you both views:

Filtered (20 MHz) view: clean waveforms for ripple numbers. This is also the convention many power-supply ripple specs use, so keep it for spec comparisons.
Raw (full-bandwidth) view: for spike hunting and peak excursions.

Each report can then show two numbers per rail, e.g. “ripple 18 mV (20 MHz) / worst-case peak +62 mV (full BW).”

Check what “my probe is 20 MHz” means

If it’s the scope’s bandwidth-limit setting: just change it in the script (CH<x>:BANdwidth) and filter afterward as above.
If the probe itself is rated 20 MHz (common for some current and differential probes): no setting will help. That probe can’t see fast spikes, and you’d need a faster one for spike hunting. Keep using it for slow, accurate measurements.

Dealing with the extra noise

Opening up the bandwidth will make the waveforms look fuzzier, and Peak Detect will make the fuzz look thicker, because it saves noise peaks too. Some ways to manage that:

Fix the source, not the symptom. A lot of “noise” on rail measurements is pickup from long ground leads, not anything on the rail. Shorter ground connections or a probe made for power rails reduce it without hiding anything real.
Zoom the vertical scale onto the rail. Use offset so a 0.9 V rail fills the screen around 0.9 V, rather than 0–12 V. The scope’s 12-bit resolution then goes to the part you care about. Leave enough headroom that big spikes don’t go off the top of the screen and get clipped.
Don’t use more bandwidth than you need. The scope offers several bandwidth-limit steps between 20 MHz and full. A middle step can cut a lot of noise while still showing real spikes.

Find the right setting by testing

Capture the same event, like a boot or a workload load-step, twice: once at 20 MHz and once at full bandwidth. Compare the peak values.

If they’re about the same, the 20 MHz limit wasn’t hiding anything on that rail.
If the full-bandwidth peaks are noticeably bigger, the filter was hiding real spikes from you.

Do this once per rail and you’ll know what each one actually needs. Record the result in your tool’s configuration so it isn’t guesswork.

How it works

The scope and DAQ connect separately. Your diff probes stay on the scope, watching the 3–4 rails you care most about for fast events. The DAQ connects with its own wires to its own measurement points, ideally on every rail. Many DAQs have differential inputs, so you can connect to the same sense points as the scope, or to different ones. Measuring a rail with both is fine as long as each instrument has its own connection.
The scope runs as normal. It’s armed with a window trigger, e.g. “fire if the 0.9 V rail leaves ±5%.”
When the scope triggers, it outputs a pulse on its AUX OUT connector on the back. You set what that output does in the scope’s settings, so check the manual for exactly how to set it to “trigger.”
That pulse goes into a spare DAQ input. To the DAQ it’s just another channel, one that’s flat almost all the time and jumps whenever the scope captures something.
Afterward, your software lines them up. “Pulse on the DAQ at 14:32:07.413” is the same moment the scope captured. So you get the detailed scope capture and the context around it: what every other rail was doing, which boot stage or workload was running.

The point of the wire is that you don’t have to trust two separate clocks to agree. The pulse is a direct physical marker, so the timing is exact.

Gotchas

The pulse may be too short for the DAQ to see. This is the sampling lesson again. If the trigger pulse lasts 1 µs and the DAQ only samples every 10 µs, the DAQ can miss the marker. Fixes: connect it to a DAQ digital input that detects edges (many can timestamp edges no matter how short), or lengthen the pulse with a simple pulse-stretcher circuit.
Ground loops. Connecting the scope, DAQ, board and PC together creates extra ground paths, which can add noise or hum to your measurements. Use one common ground point, and prefer a DAQ with isolated inputs if you can.
Connect the DAQ at the right spot. Measure at the rail’s sense points near the GPU, not at the power connector. Big currents create voltage drops along the board’s copper, so measuring in the wrong place shows a voltage the GPU never actually sees.

Is this ideal?

It’s a strong, practical setup. Two cheap additions make it much better:

Record the board’s sequencing signals on the DAQ too: enables, power-good signals, reset. Then the timeline shows the boot stages directly (“spike happened 2 ms after this rail’s power-good”), and you don’t have to work out the stages from the voltages.
Have the test software mark workload phases. When your script starts “workload X,” it can toggle a spare GPIO into the DAQ or write a timestamp. Then every event is tagged with what the GPU was doing at the time.

With those, every spike ends up with a detailed capture, a timestamp, what every rail was doing, which boot stage it was in, and which workload was running.
